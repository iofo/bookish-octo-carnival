Investment assumption

Total nominal return each year = inflation + capital gain over inflation + qualified dividend yield.

Dividend yield adds to total return on top of inflation and capital gain — raising it increases your overall return, not just its composition. Only affects the Taxable (Brokerage) option: dividends are taxed annually at capital gains rates, so only the after-tax portion gets reinvested. Traditional and Roth are unaffected, since neither is taxed on internal account activity.

The cost of waiting
What it costs, in today's dollars, to fund one day's worth of your final salary as annual retirement income — received every year of retirement, not just once — at a few different starting ages.

—

This is the cost of a self-directed, market-risk-bearing DIY strategy — it assumes your assumed return actually shows up, with no guarantee. It is not a quote for an insured annuity. A real insurer can't price a guarantee using equity-like returns, since they're on the hook regardless of market performance, so they invest conservatively and charge accordingly — a real deferred annuity for the same income, decades out, would cost meaningfully more than the figures above.

Federal tax is an estimate only: single-filer, 2026 IRS brackets and standard deduction ($16,100), with the deduction and bracket thresholds assumed to rise with inflation while the rates themselves stay fixed. It ignores state/local tax, filing status, credits, and other deductions — not tax advice. Pre-tax (Traditional) reduces that year's taxable income by the savings amount before computing tax, matching a traditional 401(k)/IRA. After-tax (Roth) and Taxable (Brokerage) both tax the full salary with no deduction — the difference between them is entirely at withdrawal, not on the way in. FICA (est.) is 2026 Social Security (6.2% up to the $184,500 wage base) plus Medicare (1.45–2.35%), applied to gross wages regardless of the contribution-type toggle. Both Federal tax and FICA are subtracted to get net take-home; FICA never affects any other figure on the page. Equalize net pay (Roth/Taxable only): reduces the contribution amount so that year's net take-home matches what Pre-tax would give at the same savings-rate schedule. The Savings % and Savings amount columns reflect this adjusted, smaller contribution — which is why Roth/Taxable balances often end up lower than Traditional ones for the "same" paycheck impact. Taxable (Brokerage) withdrawal tax: assumes a buy-and-hold account where gains are only realized at withdrawal, taxed as 2026 long-term capital gains (0%/15%/20% brackets, inflation-adjusted) plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax above $200,000 MAGI (a fixed threshold — by law it has never been inflation-indexed). Each year's withdrawal is assumed to carry gain in the same proportion as the account's overall gain-to-balance ratio; your original contributions come back tax-free as return of basis. 401(k) contribution limit: Pre-tax and Roth are capped at the 2026 IRS employee deferral limit ($24,500, plus a $8,000 catch-up from age 50, rising to an $11,250 "super catch-up" for ages 60–63 before reverting to $8,000 at 64+), all inflation-adjusted — if your savings-rate schedule calls for more than that in a given year, the excess simply isn't contributed. Taxable (Brokerage) has no such legal limit and always follows the full schedule.
